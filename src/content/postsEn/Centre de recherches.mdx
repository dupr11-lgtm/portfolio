## Starting point

Montserrat, the Soufrière Hills volcano. A site left partly uninhabitable by an eruption in 1997.
Putting a research centre as close to the volcano as possible was hardly an obvious choice. It was exactly this tension, observing from inside the danger, that interested me.
I built the project around two states: ordinary life on the surface and an underground refuge for when the alert sounds. It is neither a normal residence nor a conventional bunker, but a space made for living with instability.

## Walk-through

Building with instability.
The Soufrière Hills volcano changed Montserrat for good.
Think of the centre as two realities, one on top of the other: everyday life on the surface, shelter below ground.
Bring together research on wildlife, plants and climate, and keep risk at the heart of it: ash, pyroclasts, lahars.
At the first sign of an alert, everything changes.
The bunker becomes the main architecture: buried to filter the air and give protection, but pierced by a rooflight to bring in daylight.
The refuge has two levels: rest and washing, then shared living and endurance.
The whole thing runs off-grid: solar panels, batteries, filtered rainwater.
Don’t resist the territory. Find a way of staying there.

## Critical reflection

The original idea holds up: building where the risk is greatest, the better to observe it. But the project does not fully follow it through.
The bunker soon takes over. There is less talk of research than of survival. That shift is interesting, but the project does not quite own up to it.
The rooflight is the giveaway: keeping daylight under a rain of ash is as seductive as it is fragile. The project conjures up an extreme scenario without facing all of its violence. The sensory experience is there, but at the price of holding the outside at arm’s length.
